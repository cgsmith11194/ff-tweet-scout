$HIMS and $LFMD sell a commodity. You can buy the same pill from Ro, Noom, your local pharmacy or a dozen other telehealth apps. No moat. No differentiation. That’s the bear case. It’s also exactly why the winner could be enormous. 1. Same pressure, same response Hims went from 2.89M to 2.79M subscribers in Q3. Gross margin fell from 63.8% to 57.2%. Gross profit still hit a record $509M. LifeMD cut marketing 34% quarter over quarter, and subscribers held at ~350K. Management admitted some earlier cohorts underperformed, then changed pricing, plan length and the tech platform. Both are pulling back on paid acquisition and chasing retention. This isn’t saturation. At current product economics, buying the next customer with ads is often a bad use of capital. Both companies have reached the same conclusion: own a deeper, longer relationship with the patient. 2. $SPOT and $NFLX were here in 2022 New streamers kept launching, catalogs overlapped and acquisition was expensive. Investors asked what made $SPOT and $NFLX special if anyone could distribute content. That was the wrong question. Their edge was delivering a slightly better experience to millions of people, consistently, and reinvesting the cash to keep improving it. They owned the customer relationship. That gave them behavioral data, which improved personalization, engagement and retention. A small improvement looks irrelevant on its own. Across millions of users it raises engagement, which creates more learning, which makes the experience better again. Over years, that compounding is nonlinear. None of this is specific to entertainment. 3. The current healthcare model is fragile Today the sector is valued as: Gross profit ≈ pills sold × profit per pill With off-patent drugs, the molecule is identical everywhere. Competition comes down to price, convenience, trust, access and service. Patents, manufacturing complexity and regulation can still protect some margins. The margin from simply distributing a commodity is hard to defend. 4. The counterintuitive part The winner may be the platform that collapses medication margins, not the one that defends them. If you can monetize the relationship, you can sell the pill cheaply. A cheaper pill means more patients, more engagement, better retention and a more valuable intelligence layer. Low margins become a weapon. Gross margin % isn’t the metric. Absolute free cash flow is. Hims already shows the gap: margin % down, gross profit dollars at a record. $NFLX’s 2022 ad tier was read as weakness. It lowered the price of entry, reduced friction for customers and squeezed competitors. The rest is history. 5. From pills to tokens Old model: acquire a patient, prescribe, earn a margin on the transaction. New model: build a longitudinal record of biomarkers, symptoms, treatment responses, side effects, adherence and outcomes, then use it to train AI that personalizes care. The objective becomes maximizing measurable patient outcomes per dollar. The flywheel: More patients → more longitudinal data → better AI → better outcomes per dollar → more adoption and retention → repeat Healthcare raises the stakes. $NFLX helps you pick a movie. A health platform could pick the intervention most likely to improve your physiology, so personalization is worth far more. You can get the pill anywhere. The intelligence about which intervention works for which patient, at what dose and in what combination, is the differentiated product. New equation: relationships × recurring value per relationship. AI tokens are one input. The goal isn’t to sell tokens. It’s to turn ever-cheaper intelligence into ever-more-valuable outcomes, and capture the value through subscriptions, care services and the marketplace. 6. Owning the patient flips the supply chain Patients want outcomes. Manufacturers want patients who start treatment, stay on it and get results. The platform sits in the middle. If it improves outcomes, both sides pay for access. New drugs and suppliers plug into the existing network, the way $SPOT adds music and $NFLX adds shows. Healthcare is harder. It needs clinical judgment, privacy and validation, and more data isn’t automatically better. Those same constraints make the capability harder to copy once someone builds it. A competitor can source the same pill. It can’t easily copy millions of longitudinal patient relationships, the clinical infrastructure and validated treatment intelligence. That’s the moat. 7. Why Hims Millions of relationships, plus clinical services, pharmacy, diagnostics, multiple categories and AI-assisted care. In Q3, its AI-native experience reached 70% of weight-loss subscribers, and those patients sent 4x as many messages. That’s early engagement. The real test is whether engagement produces better data, better outcomes and better economics. LifeMD is racing for the same goal: prescribing, pharmacy, labs and longitudinal care in one place. The winner won’t be whoever has the biggest ad budget or the cheapest GLP-1. It will be whoever turns its network into a self-reinforcing edge in experience, outcomes and cost. 8. How to spot the winner Organic subscriber growth: less dependence on paid ads Engagement per patient: the relationship is getting stickier Multi-condition adoption: the platform becomes the patient’s healthcare home LTV vs CAC: the engagement shows up in unit economics FCF per share: shareholders actually capture the value The confirming signal is engagement up, retention up and FCF per share up, while medication margins fall. 9. How this could fail Engagement doesn’t improve outcomes The data doesn’t enable real personalization Patients keep switching on price Clinical AI becomes a commodity available to everyone Those are the things to test over the coming years. Bottom line The market is pricing the distribution of interchangeable pills. The bigger opportunity is owning the patient relationship and the intelligence it produces. Scale × engagement → proprietary data → better intelligence → better outcomes per dollar → more adoption → recurring revenue → FCF per share Anyone can sell the pill. The winner will own the patient.
