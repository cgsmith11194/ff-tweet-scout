The interesting question I found when researching for this post is not whether Bruno fits in at Arsenal. His qualities clearly do. The real question was how Arteta will/should use him. Key USPs Bruno is primarily a pass-first progressor. He constantly scans before receiving, understands where the pressure is from and positions his body well to play forward as early as possible. He is comfortable receiving from the CB, between the lines or with an opponent on his back, using his close control and press resistance to create the separation required for the next action. His first instinct is rarely to circulate possession without purpose. Bruno looks to punch vertical passes into a forward’s feet, release runners through or over the defensive line and use sharp third-man combinations to get around the opposition midfield. He can also play diags with his low backfoot technique, particularly after regains when the opposition structure is unsettled. That separates him from several of our current options. MLS is more carry-first, Rice progresses through physicality and vertical running, while Ode controls tempo through shorter combinations. Zubi is also a pass-first line-breaker, but he generally operates from deeper areas and prioritises control. Bruno can carry when required (69th percentile for prog carries), but his instinct is to move the ball forward earlier and with more venom. He ranked in the 93rd percentile for forward passes, 91st for passes into the final third and 89th for progressive passes. The key translation question is how much of that output survives against our usual opposition. A meaningful share of Bruno’s most dangerous progression came in open or transitional moments, whereas we regularly face compact low blocks with fewer vertical gaps. His percentile rankings still reflect quality, but also role and team context. Bruno should improve the speed and intent of our progression, but consistently creating against settled low blocks will be the real test. The Likely Tactical Fit: Bruno G in the RHS Why the RHS makes sense The most likely structure would have Bruno starting on the right of the pivot, Rice operating from the left and Ode playing ahead while naturally gravitating towards Saka. I understand why people expect that. Bruno has spent most of his recent career occupying the RHS and produced one of the best attacking campaigns of his Newcastle career from there last season (9 goals and 5 assists). His heat map, pass map and final-third actions map all lean towards the right side. The body orientation is also natural. When Bruno receives in the RHS, his dominant right foot opens towards the centre of the pitch. He can see Saka, Ode and the CF without making a major body adjustment, allowing him to play into feet, find runs through passing lanes or combine with Ode before progressing further. The technical quality of a Bruno G, Ode and Saka relationship would be ridiculous. Bruno could operate slightly deeper than Ode in the RHS, giving us two different progressive profiles around Saka. Ode controls the tempo through shorter combinations, while Bruno is more willing to break the defensive line early and play that direct pass. The impact on the Left Winger The LW would still receive, although often through a more indirect way. The RHS would be loaded, dragging the opposition block across and then Bruno’s can play diagonals to find the LW in isolation. The concern for me is that the LW may become overly reliant on those diagonals. Even when the switch creates a 1v1, the winger could receive without the close combinations and early vertical service available on the RHS. The effectiveness of the structure would therefore depend heavily on his ability to create independently. That concern applies most clearly to Martinelli, whose qualities are maximised when he can attack space before the defence resets. Tzolis is also direct, but his stronger carrying and chance-creation profile may make him more comfortable receiving in isolation. Bruno as the advanced right sided #8 Bruno G could also replace Odegaard as the right #8. In that scenario, Zubi would probably need to start deeper and establish rhythm while Bruno plays more directly around Saka. Zubi is exceptional at circulating possession safely, controlling the tempo and providing the structural stability that allows others to take more risks. Thus, having Zubi behind Bruno G would allow him to become the more aggressive creator. He would constantly look to release Saka or the CF rather than carrying the responsibility of controlling every phase of possession. The difference between receiving early against an unbalanced defender and receiving after three or four circulation passes is obviously massive. Rice sits in the deep left channel, Timber inverts from the right and Bruno remains higher in the LHS alongside Ode in the RHS. Rice protects the centre and transitions, Timber supports circulation, Bruno progresses from the left and Ode continues controlling the RHS around Saka. Rice can provide the structural floor in early build-up with Ode dropping deep to receive too, while Bruno receives closer to the opposition midfield. The Bruno x Calafiori x LW relationship One of the most exciting parts about Bruno in the LHS is the relationship that it could create between Bruno, Calafiori and the LW. Xhaka comparison and the key trade-offs of Bruno G in the LHS Any comparison to Xhaka should be positional rather than stylistic. Xhaka’s left foot gave him natural access towards Martinelli and the touchline, while much of his 2022/23 value came through late box arrivals rather than acting as the primary progressor. Bruno could occupy a similar advanced LHS area, but the role would function differently: he would offer more involvement in build-up and more aggressive progression, while providing less natural access towards the LW and probably fewer repeated runs beyond the ball. Depending on Calafiori’s positioning, Bruno may be asked to create from behind the ball more often than Xhaka did in 2022/23. There are two clear trade-offs with Bruno in the LHS. Firstly, Bruno’s heavy one footedness could occasionally delay passes towards the touchline because his body naturally opens towards the centre rather than the LW. Against an aggressive mid-block, that delay could allow the RB to close the space or pressure to arrive from Bruno’s blind side. His trivela passing can provide an alternative, but it should not be treated as a repeatable replacement for the left footed access Xhaka offered. His ability to win 2.75 fouls per 90 offers some protection under pressure, but it does not completely solve the receiving-angle issue. The second clear trade-off involves Rice. If Bruno remains advanced and Calafiori pushes forward, Rice would have to stay deeper more often, restricting his dynamic ball-carrying and late box entry. I believe the roles would therefore need to rotate: when Bruno drops, Rice can advance; when Rice runs beyond him, Bruno can hold the deeper left space. Why the trade-off is worth it for me: - Earlier service into the LW - Better combinations around Calafiori - Symmetrical attacking threat - A potentially elite rest-defence base, provided Rice and Timber remain disciplined behind the ball. Attacking Output and Creativity Bruno scored 9 PL goals from 5.60 xG and recorded 5 assists from 4.98 xA, leading Newcastle in both categories despite missing 63 days last season. He scored those 9 goals from only 42 shots, producing a 21% goal conversion rate (17.5% non-pen) and a 55% SOT rate (23 on target). His 7 non-pen goals came from 4.02 npxG, while all his attempts produced 8.57 xGOT from 5.60 xG, showing strong shot placement last season, although that level of overperformance may not be fully repeatable. Bruno isn’t a particularly high volume shooter. He averaged 1.54 shots per 90, but 0.84 of those efforts hit the target. The low volume and high SOT rate (55%) suggest a relatively selective shooting profile. However, the finishing was not perfect. Bruno scored only 4/8 big chances across all comps and only 2/6 non-pen big chances. He can certainly miss a clear opportunity, particularly in the air, but the overall data still suggests genuine midfield goal threat through late arrivals, second balls and shots from the edge of the area. Creatively, he recorded 5 assists from 4.98 xA, created 7 big chances and averaged 1.65 key passes per 90. He is not an elite final-action specialist. His creativity often comes one action earlier: - Line-breaking passes - Increasing speed of progression - Creating better receival situations for the player who makes the final pass That distinction is important. Bruno may not consistently record the assist, but his pass can eliminate the opposition midfield and leave another player attacking the defensive line. His willingness to play forward before the obvious passing lane appears is a major part of his creative value. Bruno also completed 2.97 accurate long balls per 90 (90th percentile) at 60.4% accuracy. It gives us another midfielder capable of changing the point of attack without needing 5+ circulation passes. Defensive Profile The other major reason I love the fit is Bruno’s aggression. He enjoys contact, attacks loose touches with real intent and reacts immediately after turnovers. He won 6.16 duels and made 2.27 tackles per 90 in the Premier League, while winning possession in the final third 0.66 times per 90. His value is clearest when he regains the ball and immediately looks forward. Bruno can turn a defensive action into a vertical pass or diagonal switch before the opposition block reorganises. However, that instinct is not an automatic fit for us. Arteta’s mid-block is positionally strict. Bruno’s tendency to leave his zone and attack the receiver can either kill the transition or open space behind him if the pressing trigger is wrong. He may therefore need to channel that aggression more selectively. The key adaptation will be aligning his jumps with our pressing triggers without removing the front footed defending that makes him valuable. There is also a disciplinary cost. Bruno committed 1.65 fouls per 90 and collected six Premier League yellow cards. He can become emotional and occasionally jump into challenges too early. I would still rather manage that aggression than continue lacking it, but its usefulness will depend on how well his pressing decisions align with the structure around him. Weaknesses Despite being excellent under pressure, he is not always secure. He was dispossessed 1.69 times per 90 (19th percentile). He can occasionally hold onto possession for too long, force overly ambitious passes or look for soft fouls that aren’t always given. Further, his duel volume also looks stronger than his efficiency. Winning 6.16 duels per 90 (79th percentile) is impressive, but his overall success rate was 50.5% (38th percentile). He competes constantly, which increases the number of duels he wins, but he is not completely dominant within those situations. For me his biggest weakness is his aerial ability. Despite being around 6”0, he won only 0.48 aerial duels per 90 (10th percentile) at a dismal 37.1% win rate (15th percentile). He should not be viewed as a reliable aerial outlet or somebody who consistently dominates direct balls. Bruno is not an explosive recovery athlete either. Gradient Sports rated him at 16.9/100 for athleticism. His data recorded a top speed of just 31.1 kmph and only 5.15 sprints per 90. His transition defending relies more on anticipation, aggression and elite positioning awareness compared to quick recovery speed. If he jumps out and fails to win the ball, the players behind him may have to cover a large space. There was also a notable drop in his UCL efficiency. His long-ball accuracy fell from 60.4% in the PL to 39.3%, while his duel success dropped to 41.4%. The seven-game sample and Newcastle’s wider performances provide context, but the decline against stronger opposition is still worth noting. These are genuine limitations rather than deal-breakers, but they should influence the role he is given and the structure Arteta places around him. Versatility Bruno is primarily an #8, but he can operate across the entire midfield. He is comfortable in either half-space, can drop in as the #6 and has the intelligence to adjust his positioning depending on the structure around him. His best qualities are still maximised as an #8, where he can progress, create and compete higher up the pitch. However, his ability to cover every midfield role gives Arteta valuable flexibility without requiring a major tactical change. Leadership Note Bruno’s leadership is also a cool aspect. As we know, Arteta has consistently targeted players with captaincy experience or prominent roles within leadership groups. Bruno captained Newcastle, Rice captained West Ham, while Odegaard captains both Arsenal and Norway. That would give us a midfield containing 3 players with major leadership experience. Bruno is vocal, aggressive and willing to take responsibility during difficult moments, which is exactly the kind of personality Arteta values in high pressure situations. He provides pass-first progression, attacking output, aggression, leadership and the flexibility to operate across our midfield. The RHS probably maximises his familiarity and may be his individually optimal position. However, my personal preference remains the LHS because that is where I believe he improves our overall balance the most. The concerns surrounding the adaptation to the LHS and his high-risk playstyle are genuine, but I believe they can be managed within the surrounding structure. A midfield of Rice, Guimaraes and Odegaard would offer technical security, creativity, aggression and personality. It is a concept that seriously excites me.
