Out of 100 Tries, the Robot Got 22 Right. Then the Way It Learned Changed. Picture a robot arm in a warehouse. A box slides toward it, and it reaches. Out of every 100 tries, 22 end the way they should. The other 78 end with a dropped box, a missed grasp, or a person walking over to reset the whole thing. Nobody trusts a machine like that, and nobody should. A robot that fails 78 times out of 100 is a demo, not a coworker. You would never hand it a real job, and you would certainly never leave it alone with something that matters. Now picture the same kind of robot a while later. Out of 100 tries, it gets 52 right. It is still not perfect, and I am not going to pretend it is. Nearly half of its attempts still fail. But the number moved by 30 points, and nobody rewrote its brain by hand. What changed was the way it learned from data. Those two numbers, roughly 22% and 52%, come from research published by @axisrobotics . They sit at the center of something bigger than the usual "we collected X million trajectories" headline. Let me walk you through it slowly, in plain language, because the idea underneath is easy to miss and it changes how you should think about what a contributor adds. A Pile of Runs Is Not the Same as a Library A trajectory is one recorded run of a robot doing a task. It holds the joint states, the actions taken, where the objects were, and whether the run succeeded or failed. That is useful, but it is limited. Here is the limit. Once a policy has seen enough versions of "pick up the cup", more identical picks teach it very little. The thousandth cup on the same table in the same lighting is almost a copy of the first. Counting those copies makes the total look impressive without making the robot much smarter. What scales better is something else. You pull a skill out of those runs, test it under new conditions, and chain it with other skills. A skill is a reliable piece of behavior, like grasping a handle or placing an object on a target. Once you have enough of them, you can combine them into longer jobs the robot was never recorded doing from start to finish. That is the shift Axis describes in its research: moving from raw trajectories to reusable, composable and distillable skills. In plain words, they want a growing library of things a robot can reliably do, not just a growing pile of recordings. Think of the difference between a drawer stuffed with loose photographs and a shelf of labelled books you can look things up in. The Three Findings, Explained Simply Axis reported three results in this research. I will take them one at a time and say what each one means in everyday terms. 1. A deployed robot can help train its own successor. Axis calls this Embodied RSI. Here is how the loop works, step by step: Simulation supplies large amounts of broad training data, covering many scenes and situations. A policy trained in simulation is deployed on a real robot. That policy generates a small amount of real-world calibration data while it works. The real-world data feeds the training of the next, stronger policy. In their experiments, this raised the real-robot success rate from approximately 22% to 52%. That is the story at the top of this post. Why does the small amount of real data matter? Because simulation is never a perfect copy of the real world. Real surfaces are slipperier, real lighting is stranger, and real objects are heavier or lighter than they look. A little data from the real machine closes part of that gap, and the simulation does the heavy lifting on volume. 2. A smaller, smarter dataset can beat the full pile. Axis uses a lightweight proxy model that scores each trajectory on two things: how useful it is and how much new ground it covers. In their results, a filtered subset of the data outperformed the full unfiltered pool. Think of studying for an exam. Reading the same chapter fifty times is less useful than reading ten different chapters once. A dataset behaves the same way. Ten thousand photos of the same wall teach less than a hundred photos of different rooms. Removing the repeats and the low-value runs is not wasteful. It can make the final result better. 3. Expert skills are cheap and can be chained. Axis reports that expert models reach near 100% success on individual tasks for roughly $5 to $10 of compute per task. Those experts can then be chained into longer behaviors, such as grasp, move and place in one smooth sequence. That price matters. If producing a reliable single-task expert costs about the price of a lunch, the question stops being "can we afford to build one" and becomes "which ones do we build, and what do we train them on". Which brings the whole thing back to data. Put the three together and you get the loop Axis is aiming at: the robot acts, it generates data, the useful trajectories are filtered, the skills improve, the skills get composed, and stronger models follow. How to Read the Numbers Without Getting Carried Away A 30-point jump is large, and it deserves attention. It also deserves honest reading. The 22% to 52% figure is a reported real-robot success rate, described by Axis as approximate. A success rate of 52% means about half of attempts still fail. That is progress, not a finished product. I also could not find how many tasks or trials sit behind that number, and that detail matters a great deal when you judge a result like this. A figure from a handful of tasks tells you something different from a figure averaged over hundreds. The "cheap experts" claim is also per task. Near 100% success on one narrow task for $5 to $10 is impressive. It does not mean a robot can do everything near 100% of the time. Chaining skills into long behaviors is a stated capability, but long behaviors are exactly where small error rates add up. I am not saying this to knock the work. I am saying it because you will see people repeat these numbers as if they were final, and I would rather you be the person who read them correctly. What This Means for a Signed File This part is my reading of Axis's findings, not something they stated, so treat it as reasoning and not as a rule. If the system scores trajectories by usefulness and coverage, then not every signed file carries the same downstream value. A clean demonstration of something the dataset does not have yet adds coverage. A short correction at the exact moment a policy fails adds information the policy was missing. A long run that repeats a skill the dataset already holds adds much less. The lesson is the same one the data keeps teaching: variety beats volume. There is also a practical reason diversity matters for skills specifically. To pull a reusable component out of many runs, you need those runs to differ in the ways that matter, such as layouts, object types and starting positions. Otherwise the component only works in the one place it was recorded. Variation is what lets a skill transfer. One rule stays fixed. Only runs that pass verification and are signed on Base enter the set that Axis can curate. An unsigned completion never becomes part of the record, so it cannot be scored, filtered or reused by anything downstream.
