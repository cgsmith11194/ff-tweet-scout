Nd oxide prices fell from about $134/kg in 2022 to about $56/kg in 2024. The constraint shows up at scale and by turbine type: direct-drive permanent-magnet turbines use roughly 180 kg Nd/MW; geared turbines use 12 to 50 kg/MW. Most onshore wind is geared. The pressure point is scaled offshore direct-drive, plus EV motors (the larger growth term), plus electronics, plus defense, all drawing on the same stream. Refining and separation of neodymium is almost entirely controlled by China. Dysprosium. Production is roughly 1,000 to 2,000 tons a year. Over 1988 to 2021 the mine split was about 87 percent China and 11 percent Myanmar; separation is near-total Chinese control. Dy is a small fraction of rare earth deposits and the heavy rare earth that gives NdFeB magnets their high-temperature performance for offshore service. The 2022-to-2050 cumulative demand range of 176,000 to 318,000 tons crosses China's current stated dysprosium reserve in 2032, 2034, 2038, or 2040 under the static case, moving to 2038 to 2049 if that reserve grows at 2 percent a year. Global reserves outside Myanmar sit around 800,000 tons in that same literature; the cumulative range does not drain that stock, but it does drain the Chinese share. Not a one-buildout exhaustion story, but another concentrated supply with no non-Chinese refining at scale. What binds first. Dysprosium at scaled EV motor and permanent-magnet wind production is the first heavy rare earth to bind hard. Terbium is a concentrated supply with no non-Chinese refining at scale; lower loadings than dysprosium mean a longer exhaustion timeline, and the geopolitical dependency is identical. Byproduct of zinc refining with roughly 900 tons a year of output. A constraint on CIGS thin film and on transparent conductors, not on the dominant silicon PV path. Polysilicon. Not a scarcity problem. An energy problem (silicon purification is coal-fired in Xinjiang) and a labor problem (documented forced labor in that same region). Covered below. Copper, Aluminum, and the Grid The IEA figure of about 80 million kilometers of new transmission and distribution by 2040 is real. High-voltage overhead transmission conductors are predominantly aluminum (ACSR, AAC, AAAC). Copper dominates in distribution cabling, generator windings, EV motors, and building wiring. USGS copper reserves are about 980 million tons; mine production was about 23 million tons a year in 2024 and 2025. Ore grades have fallen over the last century. Industry shorthand puts the drop from about 2 percent copper in 1900 to about 0.5 percent today; this is a sector-wide approximation, not a USGS global time series, and the decline is real but uneven across deposits. STORAGE The wind does not blow all the time. The sun sets every night. Any intermittent-generation system needs firming. Lithium mine production reached about 222,000 tons in 2024 and an estimated 290,000 tons in 2025. USGS reserves are around 37 million tons. The resource is not immediately scarce. Each carries its own materials bill and its own siting problem. A grid-scale firming solution matched to a wind-and-solar-dominant system at full load, through winter and dark weeks, at any defensible cost, does not exist today. One day of draining muscle fuel and eating almost no carbs nearly doubled how much fat trained athletes burned at their peak. Their mitochondria didn't change at all. A new study in The Journal of Physiology helps explain where the extra fat burning comes from. Researchers at the University of Copenhagen tested 10 endurance-trained men, average age 26. Each one went through the protocol twice, once with a high-carb diet and once with a low-carb diet, in random order. A few terms matter here. Muscle glycogen is the carbohydrate your muscles store as ready fuel. Peak fat oxidation (PFO) is the most fat your body can burn per minute during exercise. Fatmax is the exercise intensity where that peak happens. Mitochondria are the structures in your cells that turn fuel into energy, and mitochondrial efficiency is how much energy they make from each unit of oxygen. The question: does low glycogen make you better at burning fat because the mitochondria themselves change, or because of something upstream? Here is what they did. On day 1, the men did a graded cycling test, then 2.5 to 3 hours of hard exercise to empty their glycogen. They then ate either about 580 g of carbs (high-carb) or about 66 g (low-carb) over the rest of the day. On day 2, they repeated the biopsy and the cycling test. Key findings: • Muscle glycogen fell 30% after the high-carb diet and 69% after the low-carb diet • PFO rose 24% after high-carb, from 0.37 to 0.46 g/min • PFO rose 90% after low-carb, from 0.39 to 0.74 g/min • Fatmax shifted from 41% to 56% of max aerobic capacity only in the low-carb condition, so peak fat burning moved to a harder effort • Free fatty acids in the blood, the fat circulating as fuel, rose from 298 to 720 µmol/L and tracked closely with PFO • Mitochondrial efficiency did not change in either condition So the extra fat burning didn't come from mitochondria becoming better at their job. It came from changes in fuel supply. With less glycogen and less insulin, more fat gets released into the blood, and muscle uses it. There are important limits. Only 10 young, highly fit men took part, and the effect lasted one day. The diets changed more than glycogen. Fatty acids, ketones, insulin, and glucose all shifted, so glycogen can't take full credit. The men also felt the low-carb test was harder (effort rating 16.5 vs 13.6) and their max power dropped about 7%. The authors note the study was likely underpowered to detect small changes in mitochondria. What this means for training for longevity: • Your fat-burning number isn't fixed. It shifts within 24 hours based on what's in your muscle and blood, so a lab test or wearable estimate reflects your recent fueling as much as your fitness. If you track it, keep your prior-day eating consistent. • Fat burning is not the same as fat loss. This study measured fuel use during exercise, not body fat over time. Total energy balance still drives that. • A higher peak isn't automatically a better one. The low-carb condition felt harder and cut max power, so it likely isn't the way to do your hardest sessions. • The real goal is metabolic flexibility, the ability to switch smoothly between fat and carbs depending on demand. Low fat oxidation is linked to insulin resistance and weight gain, and regular aerobic training is what builds this capacity. For most people, that means building a consistent base of easy aerobic work and fueling harder sessions properly. Occasionally training with lower glycogen may be a tool, but this study doesn't show it improves long-term health outcomes, and it hasn't been tested in older adults or women. Still, it's a useful reminder that how your body uses fuel is flexible. The metabolic flexibility you build in your 30s and 40s shapes how well that switch works in your 60s and 70s.
