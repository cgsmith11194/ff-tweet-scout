THE METALS Neodymium. World rare earth oxide mine output was about 390,000 tons in 2024. Praseodymium-neodymium oxide supply was roughly 90,000 tons, approximately balanced with magnet demand. Nd oxide prices fell from about $134/kg in 2022 to about $56/kg in 2024. The constraint shows up at scale and by turbine type: direct-drive permanent-magnet turbines use roughly 180 kg Nd/MW; geared turbines use 12 to 50 kg/MW. Most onshore wind is geared. The pressure point is scaled offshore direct-drive, plus EV motors (the larger growth term), plus electronics, plus defense, all drawing on the same stream. Refining and separation of neodymium is almost entirely controlled by China. Dysprosium. Production is roughly 1,000 to 2,000 tons a year. Over 1988 to 2021 the mine split was about 87 percent China and 11 percent Myanmar; separation is near-total Chinese control. Dy is a small fraction of rare earth deposits and the heavy rare earth that gives NdFeB magnets their high-temperature performance for offshore service. The 2022-to-2050 cumulative demand range of 176,000 to 318,000 tons crosses China's current stated dysprosium reserve in 2032, 2034, 2038, or 2040 under the static case, moving to 2038 to 2049 if that reserve grows at 2 percent a year. USGS does not publish a dysprosium reserve; the Chinese figure comes from the referenced literature. Global reserves outside Myanmar sit around 800,000 tons in that same literature; the cumulative range does not drain that stock, but it does drain the Chinese share. A separate study of China's own dysprosium demand puts electric vehicles at 41 to 56 percent of cumulative demand and wind at 18 to 24 percent. Dy-reduced and Dy-free magnet research exists; it is not yet the volume product. Terbium. About 400 tons a year of production. Loadings in magnets are a fraction of Dy loadings. Not a one-buildout exhaustion story, but another concentrated supply with no non-Chinese refining at scale. What binds first. Dysprosium at scaled EV motor and permanent-magnet wind production is the first heavy rare earth to bind hard. Cumulative demand to 2050 crosses China's current stated dysprosium reserve in the 2032-to-2040 window under the static-reserve case, moving to 2038-to-2049 if that reserve grows at 2 percent a year, if magnet chemistries stay Dy-rich. Global reserves outside Myanmar (~800,000 tons) are not drained by that cumulative range, but the China-concentrated share is. Terbium is a concentrated supply with no non-Chinese refining at scale; lower loadings than dysprosium mean a longer exhaustion timeline, and the geopolitical dependency is identical. Neodymium reserves are not immediately exhausted, but the Chinese refining chokepoint can close overnight, and reserves held in uneconomic grades do not substitute for refined metal on any useful timescale. SOLAR Silver. Crystalline silicon cells use about 10 tons of silver per gigawatt, down from about 50 tons/GW roughly fifteen years ago through aggressive thrifting. World silver mine production was 25,300 tons in 2024. USGS reserves are 610,000 tons. Photovoltaics took roughly 6,000 tons in 2024, around a quarter of mine output. A 12,400 GW scaled buildout at 10 tons/GW requires about 124,000 tons of silver, roughly a fifth of known reserves. One buildout. Then rebuild every 25 years. Compounded against industrial, electronics, and jewelry demand, the pressure is real; the reserves are not immediately exhausted. Tellurium. Cadmium telluride thin film is roughly 5 percent of the module market. Modern CdTe cells use about 15 tons Te/GW. World refinery output is around 1,000 tons a year, already a hard cap on how much CdTe can be built annually. CdTe cannot scale to carry the whole market. Indium. Byproduct of zinc refining with roughly 900 tons a year of output. A constraint on CIGS thin film and on transparent conductors, not on the dominant silicon PV path. Polysilicon. Not a scarcity problem. An energy problem (silicon purification is coal-fired in Xinjiang) and a labor problem (documented forced labor in that same region). Covered below. Copper, Aluminum, and the Grid The IEA figure of about 80 million kilometers of new transmission and distribution by 2040 is real. High-voltage overhead transmission conductors are predominantly aluminum (ACSR, AAC, AAAC). Copper dominates in distribution cabling, generator windings, EV motors, and building wiring. USGS copper reserves are about 980 million tons; mine production was about 23 million tons a year in 2024 and 2025. Ore grades have fallen over the last century. Industry shorthand puts the drop from about 2 percent copper in 1900 to about 0.5 percent today; this is a sector-wide approximation, not a USGS global time series, and the decline is real but uneven across deposits. Lower grade means more rock, more diesel, more water, and more energy per ton. The scaling cost of continued extraction is where the system actually binds, not a single reserve-exhaustion number. STORAGE The wind does not blow all the time. The sun sets every night. Any intermittent-generation system needs firming. Lithium mine production reached about 222,000 tons in 2024 and an estimated 290,000 tons in 2025. USGS reserves are around 37 million tons. The resource is not immediately scarce. The pressure is on refining, cell manufacturing, and the pace of capacity growth. Backing one day of US electricity (about 11 TWh) entirely with lithium-ion batteries is impractical. That is a design choice. Pumped hydro, long-duration flow batteries, compressed air, hydrogen, and massive overbuild with curtailment are the alternatives. Each carries its own materials bill and its own siting problem. A grid-scale firming solution matched to a wind-and-solar-dominant system at full load, through winter and dark weeks, at any defensible cost, does not exist today. THE CHILDREN More than 70 percent of the world's cobalt comes from the Democratic Republic of Congo. The artisanal share of Congolese production has fallen from about 20 percent in the mid-2010s to a few percent today, but it is where the child labor sits. Children work in unregulated pits with no safety gear and die in collapsed workings for a few dollars a day. UNICEF's 2014 estimate counted about 40,000 children working in all mines across southern Congo, not cobalt alone. A 2024 Congolese government report identified 16,845 children specifically in the cobalt chain from 2019 to 2022, most between the ages of 12 and 17. Amnesty International, the Washington Post, Sky News, the BBC, and NBC have documented the artisanal cobalt labor conditions. Siddharth Kara's 2023 book Cobalt Red lays out the artisanal system in detail. The cobalt goes to trading houses, then to Chinese refineries, then into the batteries that power electric cars and store wind and solar electricity. Industrial Congolese cobalt and artisanal cobalt enter the same refining chain. Chain-of-custody auditing has improved but cannot fully segregate the artisanal fraction once it enters the mixed smelter stream. China produced about 93 percent of the world's polysilicon in 2024. Xinjiang's share of world polysilicon output has run from the mid-20s to about 45 percent depending on year. Documented forced labor of Uyghur Muslims in Xinjiang's polysilicon production has been confirmed by the US government (UFLPA), the UK government, Sheffield Hallam University, and the Associated Press. Panels built with Xinjiang polysilicon are blocked at US borders unless importers can prove otherwise. Lithium in the Atacama Desert is drawn as brine at rates documented to lower water tables and depress freshwater availability for indigenous Atacameño communities who have farmed that land for a thousand years. The water conflict is real and the communities are losing ground. The lithium goes to batteries. Nickel in Indonesia and the Philippines is clearing rainforest and running rivers red with laterite tailings. Workers die in refineries that would not pass a safety inspection anywhere in the West. Rare earth mining in Baotou, Inner Mongolia has produced a radioactive sludge lake miles across. Farmers downwind report elevated cancer rates. Livestock die. Crops fail. Baotou (the Bayan Obo deposit) is the main source of light rare earths including neodymium. The heavy rare earths that permanent-magnet offshore wind depends on, dysprosium and terbium, come from southern Chinese ionic clay deposits and from Myanmar, with their own documented environmental harms. The industry is built on this. Not as an unfortunate side effect. As a structural dependency. Separated dysprosium and terbium at volume have no non-Chinese substitute at current prices and current refining capacity; Lynas has begun separated heavy-REE output but at tons per quarter, not at scale. Polysilicon has non-Xinjiang alternatives, both non-Xinjiang Chinese capacity and non-Chinese capacity (Wacker and others), and the market is adjusting under US import restrictions, though Xinjiang still carries a large share. Cobalt has partial alternatives (Indonesia supplied about 14 percent of mined cobalt in 2025), but the artisanal Congolese stream still enters the same mixed refining chain. THE WASTE Solar panels last 25 years. Wind turbine blades last 20 years. Lithium batteries last 10 to 15 years. Wind blades are fiberglass and epoxy and cannot be melted down. US practice is landfill: blades are cut with diamond saws and buried in enormous landfills in Wyoming, Iowa, Texas, and elsewhere. Europe has moved to commercial cement-kiln co-processing, where the glass becomes raw material and the resin burns as kiln fuel. China's Solid Waste Law bans landfilling blade composites; the modeled routes are incineration, cement-kiln co-processing, pyrolysis, and mechanical recycling, but recycling capacity is still underdeveloped and not industrialized. Crystalline silicon panels contain lead in their solder and connectors. Cadmium telluride panels contain cadmium. For most panels the recycling cost exceeds the value of recovered materials. Most get crushed and landfilled, and the toxins can leach into soil. Less than 5 percent of consumer lithium batteries get recycled. EV pack recycling rates are higher where infrastructure exists, but still far from closed-loop. In 20 years the first wave of this equipment is waste. In 40 years the second wave. The piles of debris are already starting to appear.

If you’ll permit me, I’m going to write something from the heart. - - - Some of my closest friends know how deeply I love Austin. But very few people understand why. I don’t like to admit this, but I wasn’t born in Austin. We moved to Austin in 1984 when I was four years old, after my father got a call from a headhunter and agreed to leave the largest labor law firm in the Midwest and our nice life in Cincinnati to join to the Law Department at Motorola, which was a truly great American company at the time. My father bought a house my mother had never seen, in a state they’d never visited or knew anyone (other than a distant cousin), and even then the infinite sacrifice of my parents was evident for my sister Claire (18 months younger than me) and I. Even though my Dad initially worked pretty far away at the Ed Bluestein location (on South 183 not far from where the airport is now), he decided to buy a home in the Round Rock ISD, with the belief that the schools would be better than AISD by the time Claire and I needed them. It’s amazing how prescient that decision was, in retrospect. Claire and I both attended National Blue Ribbon elementary (Laurel Mountain), middle (Canyon Vista) and high (Westwood) schools. You can read about how unlikely that is here (https://t.co/NdD2iWh1Gz) (TL:DR: It’s basically a 0.0034% chance in the U.S. to attend all three). My childhood was idyllic. We were not wealthy, but we were comfortable. My father is the first person to attend and graduate from college in his family. His father worked three jobs to put him through Lehigh University (a baby Ivy known for their engineering school). My mother’s parents both attended and graduated from the University of Pittsburgh (#HailToPitt) and my mother graduated from Mount Union College in Alliance, OH. They both grew up in Pittsburgh (the cause of my intense #HereWeGo Steelers devotion), and went to rival high schools, meeting on a blind date and dating long distance throughout college in a very sweet love story. After college, Dad immediately became an officer in the Coast Guard, so they got married on 12/26/70 as he had one day off from Coast Guard duties, and this year will celebrate their 56th (!!!) wedding anniversary. My parents ran a tight ship. How cool was I in high school? My mother ran the sober prom committee. Need I say more? But I played basketball all four years and worked for my excellent high school newspaper, where I began a lifelong love for journalism and benefitted from the mentorship, care and love of our journalism teacher, Judy Gaines. My basketball season my senior year was akin to the film Hoosiers. Westwood had never won more than one playoff game in school history. That season, we improbably won four playoff games, decisively defeating two-time State Champion and undefeated #1 ranked Dallas Carter at the Ferrell Center in Waco. We ended up losing in the state semifinal to Midland Lee and my athletic career came to an end. I did have a consequential performance against Tomball in our first round playoff game, scoring 7 points in four minutes and erasing a 16 point deficit in the 3rd quarter. We won in overtime on a buzzer beater and our playoff run continued. I was carried off the court with people chanting my name, a truly bizarre, yet memorable experience. I spent part of my spring visiting colleges with my father. My top list of schools was: Notre Dame, Northwestern, Washington & Lee, and Rhodes College. I was rejected by all. My fallbacks (arrogant term, I know) were: Lehigh University (my Dad’s alma mater), UT and Texas A&M. I got into all three. I might have been able to walk on to the Lehigh basketball team, but was afraid of the brutal winters, isolated campus, and uphill walk everyday (literally). Texas A&M didn’t seem a fit for me. So I reluctantly accepted UT, mildly depressed that I was not ‘going away for school’. Almost overnight, I began having what would become an extraordinary experience in my five (victory lap!) years at UT. I joined a mid-size blue collar fraternity (Delta Chi), served as pledge class president, rush chair, social chair, and President for two years. I was Interfraternity Council (IFC) President. I was accepted into UT’s most elite men’s service and leadership organization, Texas Cowboys. I ran two successful Student Body President campaigns. And I was appointed to the President’s Student Advisory Committee (PSAC), and got to know and remain friends with the legendary former UT President Dr. Larry Faulker (we both attend Covenant Presbyterian with our brides). I don’t mention these things to relive former glories. I mention them to explain why I am eternally indebted to Austin, and to my parents for their love and sacrifice.
